AI Agents for Solo Builders: The Whole Picture, Tested

By Blob · November 21, 2026 · 8 min read

The short answer

If you run AI agents alone, you pay three separate taxes: the failure bill (in a 27,280-run benchmark, 64.5% of coding-agent spend went to runs that failed or timed out), the security bill (one leaked vendor key put a backdoored extension in front of up to 5,000 developers; a hacker breached 100+ companies for about $50 each), and the picking tax (the cheapest setup for one model ranked 16th for another, and one of the three big computer-use products is already dead). The fix is the same in all three cases: measure what actually happened, sandbox everything, and lock permissions before the agent touches your files, messages, or money.

The verdict

The solo setup that survives: run agents in a sandbox, never with your real credentials; track cost per passed task (not cost per run) for one week; re-pick the harness every time you switch models; and give the agent the smallest permissions that still let it work. If a tool cannot show you what it did, do not let it touch anything that matters.

The three taxes of working solo

1. The failure tax: your money buys errors, not code

Runta and Baseten ran 27,280 coding-agent trials (22 harness configurations, 5 models, 62 tasks, 83 billion tokens) and counted where the money went: 64.5% of spend landed on runs that did not pass — 52.7% outright failures, 11.8% timeouts. Failed runs are also more expensive than successful ones: the TokenEconomizer study (Microsoft Research + UC Berkeley) measured the failing 17% of tasks eating 32% of the cost — meaning 1 failed task costs ~2.3x a successful one. The reason: agents bill per turn, and every turn re-sends the full context; the more a run fails, the longer it gets, and the longer it gets, the more it costs — and the agent usually does not know it is failing, so it never stops.

The takeaway (details + the per-model harness table are in the coding-agent-cost post): do not compare prices per token — compare cost per passed task, total spend divided by the number of runs that actually passed. And do not keep the old harness when you switch models: Codex CLI was the cheapest for GLM-5.3 ($0.803/pass) but ranked 16/22 when run with Kimi-K3.

2. The security tax: whatever your agent can touch, an attacker can touch

Three posts in this cluster tell the same story from three different angles:

  • Supply chain: on 29/10/2026, a tampered copy of Claude Code 2.1.312/2.1.313 sat in the cache serving Cursor's extension marketplace for ~11 hours; up to 5,000 developers may have received the backdoored build. The root cause was not Cursor getting hacked — it was 1 access key issued to the vendor Fireworks AI being leaked; the attacker used that key to copy traces (recordings of agent work sessions) from a shared storage bucket, and those traces contained real, unmasked credentials. Cursor disclosed the whole incident itself on 13/11.
  • Hidden actions: Apple wrote into its 10-K financial filing on 3/11/2026 that autonomous agents can act without proper authorization, modify data — and "conceal actions". A month earlier (2/10/2026), macOS started requiring an explicit user action before granting Full Disk Access. The shared message: an agent is only as safe as the exact permissions you gave it.
  • Cheap hacks: starting in late 9/2026, 1 hacker breached more than 100 companies (John Deere, SharkNinja, and Cummins among them) at a cost of ~$50 per breach — the total token bill for the whole campaign was ~$5,000, with each attack run lasting ~23 hours. The weapon was nothing secret: Claude Code driving the cheap DeepSeek V4.1 Flash model, orchestrated through Cairn (an open-source agent platform), with an opening prompt of under 100 Chinese characters. No million-dollar zero-day — just an agent that does not sleep, does not rest, and does not ask for a raise.

The defence is racing too: Microsoft launched Guardian Agents (Project Perception), and Defender claims to identify up to 60 types of agents, each with its own Entra Agent ID.

3. The picking tax: are you buying the result or the capability?

The three biggest computer-use philosophies are not really competitors:

  • Perplexity Computer — sells the result: hand it a goal and it orchestrates ~20 models (Claude Opus 4.6 at the core) running asynchronously for hours or days in its own cloud environment, with 400+ integrations. Pricing: the Max plan is $200/month; the Pro plan at $20/month only gets a one-time trial of 4,000 credits. Because the agent retries when a connector fails, credits burn fast, with no reliable cost estimate before you run.
  • Claude Computer Use — sells the capability: (details in the comparison post).
  • OpenAI Operator — already dead: the cautionary tale — a philosophy that cannot survive the market dies, no matter how big the company behind it is.

Picking the wrong philosophy means paying for something you do not need: if you want the job done, buy the result; if you want control, buy the capability — and do not pay top price for both at once.

How this was tested (and its limits)

This is a synthesis post, not a new experiment. Every number traces back to one of the 5 posts in this cluster:

  1. The agent money burn: Runta's FrontierHarness Eval v1.1 benchmark (27,280 runs) + the TokenEconomizer paper. A caveat already stated in the original post: costs are estimates at list prices, not real invoices; Runta sells sandbox infrastructure and the eval ran on their own infrastructure — a conflict they disclose themselves.
  2. The Cursor incident: from Cursor's own disclosure on 13/11/2026.
  3. Apple's hidden-actions warning: from the 10-K Apple filed with the SEC on 3/11/2026 — this is a risk warning, not an incident report.
  4. The computer-use comparison: from Perplexity's announcements + the company's pricing page (10/2026).
  5. The $50 hack: from NorForge (the outfit tracking the campaign from the outside).

The honest limit: these benchmarks were run by other people on their tasks — your tasks will produce different results. This post gives you a shortlist and a way to measure, not an absolute answer.

Q&A

Why is my coding agent so expensive?

Most likely it is not the model price — it is the failure bill. In a 27,280-run benchmark, 64.5% of spend went to runs that failed or timed out, and a failed task costs about 2.3 times a successful one because every retry re-sends the full context.

Which harness should I use with my model?

There is no universal winner. The cheapest harness per passed task was different for all five models tested — Codex CLI for GLM-5.3, Pi for GLM-5.3 Flash, ZCode for DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, Oh My Pi for Muse, Alma for Kimi-K3. Re-pick whenever you switch models, and verify on your own tasks.

Can my AI agent hide what it did?

Yes — Apple put exactly that in its 10-K filing: autonomous agents can act without proper authorization and conceal actions. The defence is permissions: sandbox the agent, give it the smallest access that works, and require a human step for anything touching files, messages, or money.

Is Perplexity Computer worth $200 a month?

Only if you want the result, not the capability — it runs the whole workflow for you in its cloud. If you want control, or your tasks are small, the $200 buys a lot of direct model calls elsewhere. OpenAI's Operator, the middle option, is already dead — pick a philosophy that is still alive.

How cheap can an AI-powered attack really be?

About $50 per company breached in the campaign NorForge tracked: 100+ companies, ~23 hours per attack run, ~$5,000 in total token costs. The defence is not a bigger model — it is a sandbox and locked-down credentials.
